# Armor
<!--{ "parent": "world", "order": 4 }-->

*One-pager. Armor is an [object](/equipment/) that changes how [Damage](/damage/) lands on you. It doesn't make you harder to hit, and at the top end it doesn't make the hits smaller either — it makes most of them **not have happened**.*

**Protection** is listed by damage type: `M3 I4` turns melee attacks as if the DC were 3 lower and bullets as if it were 4 lower — and a type with **no number is no protection**, which is why a gambeson is no help at all against a rifle. **Absorb (A)** is a different thing and a much stronger one: it comes off the **damage**, after the roll, stepping the result down **A** notches — two wound levels to one, one level to a bare stun check, a stun check to nothing. A good hit dilutes Protection and **cannot touch Absorb**, and `nP` doesn't touch it either; the only answer to Absorb is a bigger blow. So it is the mark of a **rigid** shell — the soft armors have none, and against anything with an `A` at all, *half the hits that land stop counting* (see [Damage](/damage/)).

**Coverage** is what the piece is actually over, and the [hit-location](/damage/) roll decides whether it was in the way at all: a ballistic vest is nothing to a leg hit, a cuirass answers the three hits in ten that land on the chest, and a gambeson's sleeves stop at the wrist. **Gap (G)** is the hole in a helmet — on a head hit, roll D10, and on **G or less the helmet simply wasn't there** (the eye slot, the open face, the gap above the gorget). **Penalty** is the ease-factor price of wearing it: Coordination in a heavy suit, Perception under a full helm.

## Everything is a trade

**Heavier is not better, it is heavier.** A suit is bought with four separate currencies and every row on the table spends them differently:

| You pay in | And it costs you |
|---|---|
| **Coverage** | what the armor isn't over, the hit-location roll will find |
| **Weight** | the suit is part of the magnitude of every [exhaustion](/exhaustion/) check you make in it |
| **Penalty** | −CO is every finesse task you attempt; **−PC is everything you fail to notice**, which is somebody else's ambush |
| **Price** | full plate is the price of a small farm, and the [Technology Level](/technology-levels/) has to be able to make *and repair* it |

**Which is why armor shrank.** Full plate was built in the century that met the first firearms, and once guns were common, complete coverage stopped being worth carrying: plate went to three-quarter, then to the **cuirass** — chest front only — and by the eighteenth century often to a **gorget**, the neck and upper chest alone. Nobody decided to be worse protected. They decided that twenty kilos to cover the places you are unlikely to be shot was a bad deal, and bought the three hits in ten that matter most for a third of the money and none of the penalty. That arc is the whole lesson of this page, and it is why the table prices coverage separately from protection.

*Designer's Note — the hundred-year gap, and why the table has a hole in it on purpose. Armor did not go away because the idea was superseded; it went away for **about a century**, roughly 1780–1900, and then came back. In that window firearms beat anything a person could wear and still march, so armor shrank to the decorative: the American Civil War fielded no uniform helmet at all, and the Crimea managed a few truncated cuirasses. Then the First World War met **shell fragments** — which are not bullets, are far more numerous, and are stopped by a steel hat — and the helmet returned within two years of the shock. Soft armor and plate carriers followed as materials allowed, and we are still in that recovery. Which means **there is deliberately no TL4 armor on this table**: plate is TL3, the ballistic vest TL5, and a GM running 1863 should read the gap as the answer rather than an omission. It also means armor's presence in a setting is something you can **compute** from its weapons, materials and money rather than assume from its genre — the four currencies above are the computation, and the hundred-year gap is the proof that they are the right four. Nothing about this is a story of progress; it is a story of a trade whose terms kept changing.*

## How you beat a man in armor

Good armor doesn't take the edge off a blow, it takes the blow out of the fight — a swarm of attackers who cannot get past `A` are not slowly wearing him down, they are doing **nothing at all**, however many of them connect. So the way to beat him is not to swing more; it is to **change the question**:

- **Wear him out.** He is carrying twenty kilos of steel and swinging a weapon in it. A long mêlée is an exhaustion check with the magnitude of the effort as its modifier ([Exhaustion](/exhaustion/)), and the suit is part of that magnitude. He isn't hurt — he's **spent**, and a Spent (3) opponent is at −3 on everything, which is how you finally land something.
- **Use what he can't see.** That −1 PC under the helm is not his problem, it is your opportunity: he is worse at noticing you, worse at Initiative, worse at the thing that would have told him about the second attacker. Armor buys protection with awareness, so **come at him from where he isn't looking.**
- **Go for the gaps.** Visor, armpit, groin, the back of the knee. **Call the shot** for an up-front ease-factor penalty and the success *is* the hit on the gap — no armor rating applies to a place the armor isn't. Every joint is an adjective, and a dagger through the visor has killed better men than you. Or just aim for the head and let the **Gap** roll decide: an open-faced helm fails half the time.
- **Find the edge of the coverage.** Most armor isn't full plate. A cuirass is three hits in ten; a vest is nothing to a leg; a gorget is the neck and no more. Read the coverage line and aim somewhere else.
- **Take him off his feet.** Grapple, trip, drag him down, pin him ([Melee Combat](/melee-combat/)). Damage never enters it, and in armor he gets up slowly.
- **Bring the right tool.** A crossbow, a war pick, a lance. Penetration beats Protection; a big DC beats Absorb.

*Designer's Note — why the D&D answer is wrong, and we can name the evidence. In the AC model a swing is full damage or nothing, so a man in plate facing a crowd dies by attrition: roll enough times and the small chance gets through, at full value, over and over. History says the opposite. Spanish companies in plate and mail fought enormous forces armed with stone and obsidian, killed on the scale of hundreds, and took **negligible** casualties — not because they dodged, but because steel was not penetrable by what was being swung at it. That is the shape ForeSight reproduces: the swarm's blows fall off the bottom of the ladder, and nothing happens. It is also, note, the same asymmetry the whole game is built to produce — one highly effective person against five ordinary ones — arriving here through equipment instead of Score. And it is not invulnerability: those companies **did** lose men, to exhaustion, to being unhorsed and cut off, to missiles finding gaps, to being dragged down and taken alive. Every one of those is a rule above. Armor's job is to remove damage as the axis of the fight; the fight then has to be won on the other axes, which is exactly where we want the friction to be.*
