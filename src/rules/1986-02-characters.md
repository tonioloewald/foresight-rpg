#### [2.0] Characters
Designer's Notes: the character is the most important object in a role-playing setting; like a television program or a motion picture, a role-playing adventure generally deals with the interactions of central characters (the protagonists - in this case the player characters) and their environment, including not only natural hazards and dire beasts, but other (perhaps non-human) characters (the supporting caste of allies, antagonists, and bystanders - the non-player characters). The rules in this section deal with the creation of both player and non-player characters.

#### [2.1] Character Generation
Designer's Notes: The Player Character (or PC) is the most important product of a role-playing system. The following rules enable you to generate a (human) character for the FS system cold, ie. without any knowledge of the other rules. Of course knowledge of the other rules helps.
Procedure: A PC in FS is generated using the following procedure. A copy of the FS character sheet is necessary.

Some comments on this stage Remember that a character should be rationalised in terms of his/her sex, ethnic background, social background, and so forth. How is it that a character of wealthy permanent background has familiarity with a great many gravities, temperatures, and environments? Perhaps he/she came from a planet beyond the Federation, with a monarchy, and worked with explorers from the Royal Planetological Society. If so, then what other skills did the character need to engage in such work? If you feel unable to cope with a character's background, then by all means roll or choose a different one. The GM may object to a surfeit of Abandoned waifs, and members of Corporate Dynasties however.

Of course this all applies at an even more basic level: a character with West African background shouldn't have blue eyes and blonde hair (unless the GM has stated that in 2073 West Africa was flooded with European refugees after the Soviet invasion... ).

Step 2: The character's inherent attributes are now determined. This may be done in one of two ways: [i) Set each of the attributes to five (5) and distribute thirty-two (32) additional points among them so that they fall within the range of five to twelve (5-12) inclusive. The resultant values are the inherent attributes possessed by the character. [ii] Roll 06+4 for, and set that value beside, each attribute, then distribute a further fifteen (15) points among them so that they fall within the range five to twelve (5-12) inclusive - without lowering the value of any attribute. As above, these are the inherent values.

Step 3: determine the character's initial age. As this will determine the extent of the character's abilities to a considerable degree, the GameMaster (GM) has considerable discretion here. For a character of relative inexperience, the starting age should be 06+ 14 years; a character of moderate ability and experience should be 010+ 19 years old; a character of greater experience should be 010+29 years old (or older still, especially in a Known Space based setting, for example). Designer's Note: there is no reason for a GM not to have characters of widely varying capability in his/her campaign ( perhaps to counteract relative experience of players if desired).

#### [2.4] Fields of Familiarity
Procedure: a Field of Familiarity is a number (usually negative) reflecting the degree of familiarity a character has with a particular situation or item. A character is assumed to be familiar with any thing he/she possesses at the beginning of an "Adventure", and with any situation he/she has previously dealt with. When faced with a new situation or item to/with which a character wishes to apply a skill, the GM decides how foreign the situation or item is the the character and assigns an N FM (non-familiarity modifier, also referred to as familiarity) to it: -3 for very strange, to -1 for somewhat similar to something the character is familiar (ie. has familiarity zero or better) with. When in doubt, make the NFM -2.

There are three ways of improving an NFM: during play, a character may grow more familiar with a situation or item, and this is reflected by improving it by one (but never above zero), every time the character achieves a QR 1 or 2 in a task involving the situation or item; after a session where a character has been assigned an NFM, that NFM is removed, assuming the character is able to continue contact with the situation or item; experience may provide positive familiarity with a situation or item with which the character has been familiar with for over a year (see Experience, directly below). Note that these last rules apply to familiarity with situations and items, not fields of knowledge. NFMs for fields of knowledge are handled differently in that they cannot be improved by die rolls, but only by expending experience points, see [2.5], [3.0]. Players may wish to record their familiarity with things in the space provided. Recording familiarity with possessions and the like is unnecessary, as it is assumed; but situations are less obvious. Examples of situations and items requiring application of NFMs: a character must use a strange Aircar to make an escape, since the controls are unusual, but not entirely unfamiliar, an NFM -1 is assigned, which is nullified once the character achieves his/her first OR 1 or 2, or finishes the adventure and can study the machine at leisure; a character brought up in modem society lands on Darkover and is unused to strange and implicitly different social conventions, which can easily result in hislher causing considerable offense, the GM assigns an NFM -3 to the society, applying to all interactions (seduction, confidence, charisma, especially) and perhaps social studies, and so forth again this can be ameliorated by good ORs, or out of game-time experimentation - but note that familiarity with Darkovan society in this case reflects figuring out how to avoid offending people, not an understanding of the society, which would be a field of knowledge on its own.

#### [2.5] Experience
Procedure: in most role-playing games there is some method by which a character's abilities may improve as a result of the training and experience obtained by that character. In ForeSight a character may be improved by expending experience points (as distinct from generation points). A character gains experience points by being played, the number thus gained depending on the duration and quality of play of the owning player.

The basic award of experience in FS is 75 experience points (or EPa) per four or five hour session of play per character. This amount may be modified at the GM's discretion along the following lines: competent, productive play should receive higher awards, but not more than an extra 25 bar in exceptional circumstances, while a similar amount should be deducted for incompetent, unproductive play. Similarly adjustments for superior and inferior role-play should be made, on the basis of whether the character's actions befitted hislher stated persona. [2.7] Psionics Psionics in FS are presented for those who wish to use them, not as an integral part of the implied GameWorld, but as a module which can be added to it painlessly. H you want to make psionics integral to the Game-World, either give them to everybody in some degree (eg. make them available to everybody, exactly like normal skills), or tie them to communications, faster than light travel, or some other all-pervasive aspect of the environment. Normally, a player character has a psionic skill if and only if it is so indicated under abilities and limitations. The GM may discuss with the player which ones he/she can have, or send word down from the mountain. The Exp value of skills reflects the cost to improve them, not to acquire them. In some places psionic characters are persecuted by society (especially if it can detect them), so players may eliminate them by paying the cost for removing any other ability or limitation. A psionic task succeeds or fails normally, but using a psionic skill always costs the- psionic character fatigue equal to the OR of the attempt, and if a OR 10 was achieved, increases hislher wound level by one. Usually, the results of psionic tasks have a base duration, which determines how long the effects of the skill's application last. [2.8] Non-Player Characters A non-player character (NPC) is a character not controlled by a player. ie .• in most cases. a character controlled by the GM. Such characters. by nature. will be far more numerous than player characters. while the GM is far less numerous than the players. This means more work for the GM. who already has to figure out how the entire Universe worksl The solution is simple: [i) Do not generate important NPCs the way PCs are generated. If this wasn't already obvious. the best way to generate an NPC is decide what attributes. skills. etc. such a character would possess. and give them to him/her. Finished. Don't bother about experience points· for NPCs either. If they survive, and the PCs bump into them again, maybe they've gotten better at sometl:ling, maybe they've been too busy.

[ii] Wing it. If the situation calls for a plumber, then he/she's probably pretty reasonable at plumbing (Diagnose PCS-15; Repair PCS-20; knows Construction). If he/she has to do anything else, then he/she's probably poor to fair at it (no skill, to PCS.10, say). Just remember the following: a PCS of 10 is not particularly good; PCS-15 is okay; PCS-20 is pretty good; and PCS-25 or better represents expertise. Most NPCs will not engage in physical labour (a lot of plumbing will be done by robots, I expect, these are covered in ForeScene), and will have low Pain Resistance and Fatigue (say 10), similarly they will avoid combat (again say 10), while they will have average capacities for other common skills (Empathy, Search, Scan, and perhaps some vehicle at 15). If an NPC does something for a living, give him/her 20, or perhaps 25, in it. Simple. Do not use these guidelines for all NPCs, just the vast majority.
Also: the stats of most NPCs, especially minor ones, are generally irrelevant, and generic values can be assumed. A number of generic NPCs are supplied for your convenience, each simply requires appropriate fields of knowledge. [iii] NPCs are not as good as PCs, at least in general. Certainly powerful villains whom the PCs have sought long and hard, should be more than a match for a solitary PC, or at least possessed of wicked henchpersons, but by and large, NPCs, having less initiative, and drive, than PCs, will possess lesser capabilities. Bear this in mind. If an NPC is to be generated, the way a PC is, then give himlher base values of 3 in all attributes except AP (which remains 5). Otherwise generate the character as per normal (roll 06+2 rather than 06+4 if the semirandom method is desired). This system may be used by players who wish to generate their characters' spouses, siblings, etc. (subject to GM intervention: "Why does your husband have such a high Handguns skill, while you claim he works as a pharmacist?"). Apart from that, less space should be required to list an NPC's capabilities than a PC's, so a (more compact) NPC record is provided.

[2.91 Merits, Demerits, and Notoriety (Optional) If a character works for somebody, or is involved in some form of pecking order, then it is a good idea for the GM to keep some numerical record of hislher situation. In ForeSight a character's status in a hierarchy can be kept track of via merit points (MPs, if you like abbreviations). When a character is seen to perform some (contextually) meritorious act, he/she will gain merit points, and if he/she is seen to perform some unmeritorious act, he or she will lose merit points (or gain demerits). Note that inaction may, in some cases, constitute either a meritorious or (more probably) an unmeritorious one.

Example: a character who is a law enforcement officer might gain MPs for preventing an armed hold-up, successfully resolving a hostage situation, or rescuing a drowning person from a river; he/she might lose MPs for not attempting to do any of the above in appropriate circumstances, or displaying incompetefICS or poor judgement in an attempt to do one of the above, or using unconventional and unsanctioned methods to do any of the above (especially if he/she fails). Merit points are in general non-transferable: one's MPs gained as a policeman count for nothing if one becomes a spaceship pilot, but some proportion of one's merit points might be convertable to a related field: a paramedic might retain half of his/her merit points if he/she became a fire-fighter (and if he/she returned to being a paramedic, he/she could have the higher of hislher previous merit as a paramedic, and half hislher merit as a fire-fighter). Merit points may accord a character benefits in terms of position and promotion, salary, choice of assignment, privileges, respect, medals, and/or trust (placed in himlher by hislher employer, and/or colleagues).

Guidelines for Merit Point Awards A character should be accorded merit points only for successful and constructive performance. An act within the line of duty, but beyond normal expectations, should be accorded 1 to 5 merit points. Eg. a policeman is expected to deal with a small scale hostage situation, but to resolve it without casualties or major mobilisation would be worth merit points. An act within the line of duty, but beyond any expectation, should be accorded 6 to 20 merit points. Situations of this sort would occur most often when unforeseen events cause a character's superiors to knowingly allocate insufficient resources to a task, and the task is successfully performed anyway; and when there is more to an assignement than first appeared. Eg. a squad is assigned to seize an ·undefended" village which turns out to be defended by a platoon of veteran troops, and nevertheless prevails; or a mathematics student sits a first year exam in which hislher lecturers have placed a "Great Unsolved Problem· as a joke, and he/she solves it without realising the joke.

Notoriety Notoriety is a special case of MPs. A character gains notoriety for being believed (by law-enforcement agencies, the mass media, and/or the public) to have performed criminal acts. A character loses notoriety points for serving the prison sentences, or whatever, accorded to people who commit the crimes the character is seen to have perpitrated. Each point of notoriety is roughly equal to a year's imprisonment in today's terms, although how this works out will vary from place to place (eg. some places today put people in prison longer for harmless sexual eccentricity than for violent rape). A given crime will accord notoriety points (NPs if you prefer) to the character suspected of having performed it roughly equal in number to the number of years of imprisonment a person today would expect to serve if tried for a similar offence in some contemporary western nation. Eg. 20 NPs for second degree murder, 5-10 NPs for armed robbery, 0.1 NPs for parkingltraffic violations. A crime whose NP total is less than one twentieth of a character's existing notoriety will not contribute to hislher notoriety (eg. a character with notoriety ~2 no longer acquires notoriety for parking violations). A character's notoriety rank (see above) determines who is after himlher, and how badly they want himlher, and also, perhaps, the size of the reward for information leading to his/her arrest. Role-playing a character in a prison is likely to be pretty dull (unless, for some reason, the authorities allow him/her to roam free on some "prison planet").
