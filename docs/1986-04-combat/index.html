[4.0] Combat

Designer's Notes: the FS combat system is designed, as are many wargames, to use a hex-grid to regulate movement, and determine ranges. Players may use miniatures, counters, dice, or whatever, to indicate their characters' positions and facings on the hexgrid,assuming you use a full-sized hex-grid. The problem of course is that most people aren't going to bother with the hex-grid, and will resolve combat by having the GM keep a rough record of what is going on, and essentially "wing it". This is okay, since the hex-grid is provided more as a method of giving a person reading these rules an idea of how the system works, rather than to force users of the system to lay out a hex-grid for every combat.

There is an intermediate possibility: the GM can record the terrain, and the positions and facings of the ~ombatants on a grid of very small hexes (or squares, If preferred), and alter them as combat proceeds.

[4.1] Set-up

Procedure: Combat in ForeSight is generally the result of previous actions which have placed the involved parties in a combat situation. A combat will have some sort of setting: a building, cave complex, forest, or whatever. The GM must decide how this is to be represented on a map which is tiled with hexagons (to regulate movement, and assist range-determination), each representing an area approximately equal to one sql,!are metre (ie. they are one metre across). In FS the setting is expressed in terms of blocking, half cover, three quarter cover, and open hexes; if the GM wishes to incorporate elevation and so forth into the system, shelhe may do so in the obvious way. A half cover hex has some sort of cover, eg. a bush, cactus plant, or table, in it. A character may enter it normally, and is treated as having haH cover when kneeling, or prone in that hex.

A thre.e-quarter cover hex has some fairly prominent sort of cover, eg. part of a tree, a pillar or doorway, in it. A character must spend an entire move action (see below) to enter such a hex, but is treated as having half cover when standing, and three-quarter cover when kneeling, or prone in that hex. A line of sight may not be traced through such a hex; nor maya prone character in such a hex trace a line of sight out of it. A blocked hex contains some impenetrable obstacle eg. a brick wall, tree trunk, or boulder, and may not ~ entered, nor maya line of sight pass through it.

A character has a line of sight from point A to point B if he/she could see point B from point A. Often the presence or lack of a line of sight will be a matter for the GM to decide, so specific rules are not provided (eg. a thick glass wall would form blocking hexes for purposes of movement and missile fire, but not for line of sight (it would stop [most] lasers since the vapourised glass would absorb and refract the beam).

The GM should also decide where the characters (player and non-player) are located, and which direction they are facing, using events leading up to the combat as a guide. Sometimes it will be stated that one side or the other has been surprised, in which case that side automatically has worse initiative in the first turn than any unsurprised side (but determines initiative with regard to other surprised sides normally), and none of its members may attempt to find cover before combat. [4.2] Combat Resolution

Procedure: Combat in FS is divided into turns, each representing three seconds of time in the GameWorld, and ceases when all sides have agreed to cease hostilities, or only one side remains functional. Each turn is divided as follows into segments, which are resolved in the order described:

[i) Initiative Determination Segment: each side determines its initiative. A representative of each side makes an Initiative roll (modified for armour visibility and wound status, etc.). A character with raised hands, or some such impediment, is assumed to automatically have OR 10 initiative. [ii] Declaration Segment: The sides declare the actions of their constituents in order of descending OR of their representatives' initiative rolls (in the case of ties, in the order of ascending Initiative PCS). [iii] Action Segment: players resolve the actions of their characters, which must either correspond to those declared by them in [ii), or be pass (or in-) actions, in descending order of their adjusted speeds (or in order of agility if equal). Note that the point in this segment during which a given character performs his/her actions is referred to as that character's action pulse.

Definition: Action Points: a character (who is not performing all or part of a pulse action) has a number of action points to spend in his/her action pulse equal to his/her net speed, if it is greater than 2, and 2 otherwise. If a character has fractional adjusted speed, then ignore fractions of less than one half, and allow fractions greater than one half to be treated as whole for determining when the character acts, and discount it for purposes of action points, or vice versa (player's choice) ego a character with adjusted speed 2.5 could act at the same time as characters with speed 3, and spend 2 action points, or wait until speed 2 and spend 3 action points - effectively fractional speeds lower than 2 are rounded off). Characters performing a pulse actions round off their speeds for purposes of deciding when they act. Net Speed: Total the armour Spd rating for all limbs (also see rules the for exoskeletons. below) and fill it in the appropriate box. Add one eighth of the character's EVA skill level, and fill this in under Armour Speed Modifier (unless it is greater than zero, in which case replace this value with zero). Determine the effect of the weight (mass times gravity) the character is carrying on his/her speed (see [2.1], but it should be recorded on the character sheet), note this modifier in the box labelled Speed under encumbrance on the character worksheet. Add this to the Armour Speed Modifier to yield the character's net speed modifier. The character's net speed equals his/her speed plus this modifier (the "+ G" has been written in for your convenience). Procedures: The actions a character can perform in combat are procedures which alter hislher position, condition, or whatever. There are two sorts of actions in FS, categorised by the time it takes to perform them. Action Point Actions cost the character a number of action points to perform. Pulse Actions take a character a number of action pulses to perform. In one of his/her action pulses a character may either [1] perform action point actions costing action points s his/her available APs; [2] perform anyone action point action (ie. as a 1-pulse action); or [3] perform one pulse worth of pulse actions (eg. complete a 1-pulse action, or a third of a 3-pulse action).

Action Point Actions Move (Costs 1 Action Point): a character (who is standing) may be moved into one of his/her front hexes (without altering his/her faCing) one, two, or three times (at most two in HY gravity, at most one in EX). A Move action costs the character performing it 0.5 Fatigue points. A prone character may Move into a front hex without altering hislher facing. Optionally: a character reduces his/her speed for purposes of determining when his/her pulse occurs (only) by the number of Move actions he/she took that pulse. A character may attempt to jump (using the Jump skill at + the character's current speed modifier) the hexes between his/her initial and destination hexes (the

latter of which must be a hex he/she could have moved to with that action normally), and succeeds on a OR 3 (4 if the previous action the character took was also a Move action). Turn (Costs 1 Action Point): a character (who is standing) may alter his/her facing by 60°, and then be placed in a front hex; be placed in a front hex, and then alter his/her facing by 60°; or have his/her facing altered by up to 180° (120° in HY gravity). In EX gravity a turn action only allows a 60° change of facing by the character. A prone character may alter hislher facing by 60°. Any Parry or Dodge action performed immediately after a Move or Turn action incurs a -2 modifier. Combat Move (Costs 1 Action Point): a standing character may be moved one hex in any direction without altering hislher facing (only into front hexes in HY/EX gravity). This may be taken as an opportunity action. Combat Turn (Costs 1 Action Point): a standing character's facing may be altered by 60°, or 120° (60° only in HY/EX gravity). This may be taken as an opportunity action. Aim (Costs 1 Action Point): a character may negate the unaimed shot penalty for an immediately subsequent shot at a target in a legitimate target hex. This may be taken as an opportunity action, but then costs 2 action points. Fire (Costs 1 Action Point): a character may fire one shot (unbracketed under ROF) at a target in a legitimate target hex. Unless fired at a target aimed at, or successfully struck by an immediately prior fire action (ie. his/her previous action, which was a fire action, with that same weapon), or immediately previously fired at without the unaimed penalty, the fire action accrues the unaimed penalty. This may be taken as an opportunity action. Fire Burst (Costs 2 Action Points): as for fire action but fires weapon with bracketed ROF. The target in this case may be a group of objects. This may be taken as an opportunity action. A character (with an appropriate weapon) may also fire a burst at an area which must comprise a number of contiguous hexes not exceeding the number of shots fired; the OR of the burst determines the number of shots which hit someone or thing (eg. OR 4 indicates half the shots hit something). each takes damage determined by a random OR and the weapon's DC (when not firing bursts; or the normal DC-2 if no "single shot" DC is given). Area fire is performed at + 1 - (the number of hexes in the area), and uses the worst size and profile modifier of any of the targets the firer desires to hit; area fire never incurs the unaimed penalty.

The damage is first allocated to one of the target's locations. Human characters, for example, may be hit in one of seven different locations (see the character worksheet). Roll 010 to determine the location (the parentheses contain the 010 roll resulting in a hit to that location); this roll may be altered if, for example, a specific shot was taken. In the case of a shot, if the location was protected by some other object, then that object, instead, is damaged (unless flimsy enough to be ignored). Eg. a character crouching behind a empty barrel is harder to hit, but is not protected by the barrel from damage, whereas a character behind a boulder could ignore "hits" to his/her legs and abdomen. The damage inflicted by the attack is determined by finding the result listed under the attack's net DC (ie. adjusted for any appropriate armour protection, range, etc.) for the OR of the attack. This damage is then reduced by the number of "A"s in the appropriate armour protection rating (zero becomes an "S" result, anything less equals no effect). See [4.4] for details of weapons, including their DCs, and armour types, including their protection ratings versus various types of attack.

The resultant damage (again, "S" counts as zero) is added to the Wound Level of the target (ie. damage to individual locations is important only with regard to special effects and armour protection; the actual damage counts, regardless of location). Note that depending on the location damaged, special effects, as cited on the character worksheet, including increased damage, may accrue. These special effects apply only if some damage actually got through, of course. If the character suffered an "S" result, or an increase in Wound Level, then he/she must then immediately make a Pain Resistance (or PRj roll (ie. achieve QR4 or better) or become stunned. A QR10 PR roll indicates that the character faints from pain (ie. falls prone, and can only perform Recover from Stun actions which are successful only on a QR2 or better). A stunned character may perform only Recover from Stun actions. Note that the modifiers for being wounded apply to the EF of PR rolls too (and a character with abdomen wounds suffers an additional negative modifier). Note also that stun weapon (with damage class suffixed by an "S") hits are affected by impact protection, and any resultant damage stuns the target, rather than increasing his/her wound level. Stun weapons will stun a human for the "damage" inflicted in hours; mammals will be stunned for a greater or lesser period, inversely dependent upon mass (as a guide, divide period for humans by animal's DT value). A living human being may be in one of six wound levels, corresponding to one of six wound status brackets as follows: [0]: Okay; the person is not currently injured, except, perhaps, superficially. [1]: LIght Wound; the character is injured, and receives a -1 modifier to all resolution attempts, and Speed. Base Recovery Time: 24 hours.

[2]: Medium Wound; the character is injured, and receives a -2 modifier to all resolution attempts, and Speed, and a -1 modifier to UCDC. Base Recovery Time: 5 days.

[3]: Heavy Wound; the character is injured, and receives a -3 modifier to all resolution attempts, and Speed, and a -2 modifier to UCDC. Base Recovery Time: 30 days. Definition: Base Recovery Time functions as follows. After a character with a given wound status spends the base recovery time resting (if outdoors, he/she must also make a survival roll) he/she should make an EF3 (no modifiers) EN roll. QR1-4 indicates his/her wound status is reduced by one. QR10 indicates it is increased by one.

Wgt: the weapon's mass in kilograms. Use: the skill appropriate to the weapon (any number indicates the number of hands required to use the weapon, one and a half indicates that the weapon may be used one or two handed, but that it does more damage two-handed). "Realistic" Damage I have received some complaints about the way damage (to characters) works in ForeSight. Since I dislike introducing special cases, rather than change the damage system I include the following optional rule. which can also be used for non-player characters and creatures by a sufficiently masochistic GM.

If a character is struck in a location not containing vital organs (ie. a limb) then if the damage from the blow exceeds the character's current wound level. then current wound level becomes the damage sustained to the limb, otherwise subtract two from the damage suffered (but do not reduce it below zero) and apply it normally (including special effects). So, in effect, in becomes harder to kill a person incapacitated by a blow to the head or trunk by hitting himlher in the arms or legs. Eg. a character currently suffering a heavy wound (wound level three) is hit by another blow (to a leg) for three damage. This does not exceed hislher wound level. so two is subtracted from thEi damage before it is applied, leaving one point of damage to incapacitate the character. Now if the damage had been suffered in reverse order (supposing the earlier wound had been taken in the chest), the character would instead be dead (this is not unrealistic, since shock causes a person's blood to withdraw into his/her head and trunk, so that a major limb wound inflicted on a healthy person will have a greater relative effect on hislher overall health, than the same wound inflicted on a person in shock) Masochistic GMs Only: for creatures and characters not having DT1, replace 'wo· with the amount of damage necessary to cause the creature/character to enter wound status two (medium wound). Also, for creatures and characters without specified hit locations, make a hit location roll anyway. and if the location rolled would have struck a human in a limb, then the target is treated as having been hit in a limb.

Additional Rules for Melee Combat Combat Harness While exoskeletons give their wearers artificially augmented strength and mobility, they are not wholly optimised for combat. As armour becomes stronger, more powerful weapons are required to punch through it. and some weapon technicians feel that powerful close combat weapons are the answer to this problem. A combat harness fits onto the arms (and, to a lesser extent, legs) of a full exoskeleton, and involves a battery of industrial lasers, high-tech glove guns, and sharp projections. When a character with combat harness makes an unarmed melee attack against somebody, he/she may use either the melee DC modifier (on top of that for the exoskeleton, and hislher own UCDC), or the beam, or impact. DC ratings of the harness used. The combat harness is, obviously, extremely deadly. For all other purposes (eg. encumberance) it counts as part of the exoskeleton it is attached to.

Knockback attacks may not be parried normally (although they may be dodged). Instead, a parry of a knockback attack is resolved as an opportunity attack (necessarily at close range) before the knockback is resolved. (This parry cum opportunity attack may not itself be parried, nor may it be a knockback attack.) Throw: This operates as a special case of the Dodge action, performed at -1, except that the attacker must be close (or at normal range if he/she is using a pole-weapon); if successful the attacker is thrown into an adjacent hex of the throwing character's choice. It may also be performed at close range as an Attack (at -2). Throws do the same damage as a kick, ie. using DC - UCDC+1 (location being determined normally). Strangle: (-3, -1 if attacker has Capture field) attacker must be close to target, and have both hands free. If successful, the attack is treated as a successful restrain action, but the target will be unable to breathe (see asphyxiation in the appendices) until the restraint is broken. Strangles are less effective against targets with armoured necks; (in general) they will have no effect if the strangle attack would not have penetrated the worst of the target's head and chest armour, had it been a normal attack.
