[2.0] Characters

Designer's Notes: the character is the most important object in a role-playing setting; like a television program or a motion picture, a role-playing adventure generally deals with the interactions of central characters (the protagonists - in this case the player characters) and their environment, including not only natural hazards and dire beasts, but other (perhaps non-human) characters (the supporting caste of allies, antagonists, and bystanders - the non-player characters). The rules in this section deal with the creation of both player and non-player characters.

[2.1] Character Generation

Designer's Notes: The Player Character (or PC) is the most important product of a role-playing system. The following rules enable you to generate a (human) character for the FS system cold, ie. without any knowledge of the other rules. Of course knowledge of the other rules helps.

Procedure: A PC in FS is generated using the following procedure. A copy of the FS character sheet is necessary. r dlllille III General Notes II Ethnic Background,_ _ _ _ __ H Social Backaround, _ _ _ _ _ __ Step 1: Determine the character's social and ethnic background (this may be done by rolling 0100 and consulting the tables provided, or choosing from among the items on the tables), and record these in the appropriate slots on the character sheet; eye colour, hair colour, complexion (by this I mean skin colour), hair texture, height, and frame (build), should be chosen by the player in accordance with his/her general intent for that character's makeup.

Handedness should be determined randomly as follows: roll 010 - a result of 1 indicates an ambidextrous character (such a character has no off" hand); a 2 or 3 indicates a left-handed character (the character's right hand is considered off"); a 4-10 indicates right handedness.

The character's sex should be decided by the player some time during this step.

The character's inherent abilities and limitations should now be determined by rolling 0100 (perhaps, if so indicated, more than once) and consulting the appropriate table. The results of this should be recorded in the appropriate slot on the character sheet.

Some comments on this stage Remember that a character should be rationalised in terms of his/her sex, ethnic background, social background, and so forth. How is it that a character of wealthy permanent background has familiarity with a great many gravities, temperatures, and environments? Perhaps he/she came from a planet beyond the Federation, with a monarchy, and worked with explorers from the Royal Planetological Society. If so, then what other skills did the character need to engage in such work?

If you feel unable to cope with a character's background, then by all means roll or choose a different one. The GM may object to a surfeit of Abandoned waifs, and members of Corporate Dynasties however.

Of course this all applies at an even more basic level: a character with West African background shouldn't have blue eyes and blonde hair (unless the GM has stated that in 2073 West Africa was flooded with European refugees after the Soviet invasion... ).

[i) Set each of the attributes to five (5) and distribute thirty-two (32) additional points among them so that they fall within the range of five to twelve (5-12) inclusive. The resultant values are the inherent attributes possessed by the character.

[ii] Roll 06+4 for, and set that value beside, each attribute, then distribute a further fifteen (15) points among them so that they fall within the range five to twelve (5-12) inclusive - without lowering the value of any attribute. As above, these are the inherent values.

Step 3: determine the character's initial age. As this will determine the extent of the character's abilities to a considerable degree, the GameMaster (GM) has considerable discretion here. For a character of relative inexperience, the starting age should be 06+ 14 years; a character of moderate ability and experience should be 010+ 19 years old; a character of greater experience should be 010+29 years old (or older still, especially in a Known Space based setting, for example). Step 7: The number of years of (part-time) education the character may have had is determined. This is

equal to the character's age (in years) minus eighteen, plus the character's years of free education (indicated on the Social Background table), plus any extra purchased via [vii] above (or zero, if this is higher).

The character then receives his/her education as follows: choose a field (of knowledge) which will not take longer to (attempt to) acquire than the character's (remaining) years of part-time education (NB: a field may have other prerequisites which must be met to acquire it), subtract the number of years required to learn the field from the character's (remaining) years of part-time education, and roll
0100. H the result is less than or equal to the character's IN x the field's EF the character acquires it (record the name of the field under Fields of Experience). Failure means that the field was not acquired (record it under Fields of Knowledge with an lOp beside it), but subsequent attempts to learn it will take half as long as normal. Repeat the process until no more fields are desired, or the character has "spent" all his/her years of education.

#### [2.4] Fields of Familiarity
Procedure: a Field of Familiarity is a number (usually negative) reflecting the degree of familiarity a character has with a particular situation or item. A character is assumed to be familiar with any thing he/she possesses at the beginning of an "Adventure", and with any situation he/she has previously dealt with. When faced with a new situation or item to/with which a character wishes to apply a skill, the GM decides how foreign the situation or item is the the character and assigns an N FM (non-familiarity modifier, also referred to as familiarity) to it: -3 for very strange, to -1 for somewhat similar to something the character is familiar (ie. has familiarity zero or better) with. When in doubt, make the NFM -2.

There are three ways of improving an NFM: during play, a character may grow more familiar with a situation or item, and this is reflected by improving it by one (but never above zero), every time the character achieves a QR 1 or 2 in a task involving the situation or item; after a session where a character has been assigned an NFM, that NFM is removed, assuming the character is able to continue contact with the situation or item; experience may provide positive familiarity with a situation or item with which the character has been familiar with for over a year (see Experience, directly below).

Note that these last rules apply to familiarity with situations and items, not fields of knowledge. NFMs for fields of knowledge are handled differently in that they cannot be improved by die rolls, but only by expending experience points, see [2.5], [3.0].

Players may wish to record their familiarity with things in the space provided. Recording familiarity with possessions and the like is unnecessary, as it is assumed; but situations are less obvious.

Examples of situations and items requiring application of NFMs: a character must use a strange Aircar to make an escape, since the controls are unusual, but not entirely unfamiliar, an NFM -1 is assigned, which is nullified once the character achieves his/her first OR 1 or 2, or finishes the adventure and can study the machine at leisure; a character brought up in modem society lands on Darkover and is unused to strange and implicitly different social conventions, which can easily result in his/her causing considerable offense, the GM assigns an NFM -3 to the society, applying to all interactions (seduction, confidence, charisma, especially) and perhaps social studies, and so forth -again this can be ameliorated by good ORs, or out of game-time experimentation - but note that familiarity with Darkovan society in this case reflects figuring out how to avoid offending people, not an understanding of the society, which would be a field of knowledge on its own.

#### [2.5] Experience
Procedure: in most role-playing games there is some method by which a character's abilities may improve as a result of the training and experience obtained by that character. In ForeSight a character may be improved by expending experience points (as distinct from generation points). A character gains experience points by being played, the number thus gained depending on the duration and quality of play of the owning player.

The basic award of experience in FS is 75 experience points (or EPa) per four or five hour session of play per character. This amount may be modified at the GM's discretion along the following lines: competent, productive play should receive higher awards, but not more than an extra 25 bar in exceptional circum-stances, while a similar amount should be deducted for incompetent, unproductive play. Similarly adjust-ments for superior and inferior role-play should be made, on the basis of whether the character's actions befitted his/her stated persona. The upshot of this is that a character will receive 25 to 125 experience points per five or so hours of play.

Acquiring Fields of Knowledge

As well as improving the skills and familiarities of their characters, players will also wish to expand the fields of knowledge possessed by their characters, ie. gain fields of knowledge in addition to those acquired in character generation [2.11. For a character to acquire a field of knowledge he/she need expend no experience points, but must instead devote about four hours per day for the time specified for that field (adjusted, perhaps, if the character has an exceptional memory, or has previously failed to acquire the field being studied); a character may thus go about his/her normal business while studying no more than one field.

At the end of the stipulated time the player owning the character makes an IN roll at the EF listed for the field. The OR of the roll determines whether or not the character has successfully gained that Field of Knowledge and also the NFM (non-familiarity modifier) associated with that field for the character. The NFM is, assuming success, that listed for the field (a negative number) + 1 if a OR 2 was achieved, + 2 if a OR 1 was achieved (but not if it makes the NFM positive, in which case raise it to zero). A OR 7 indicates failure, but decreases the time required to acquire the field subsequently to half the normal time; while a OR 10 indicates nothing has been learnt, and does not affect subsequent attempts to acquire the field.

Note that this procedure does not correspond to that 'in character generation, and it is not recommended that it be used there.

Unlike NFMs for situations or items (Fields of Familiarity
[2.4]), these modifiers may only be improved by experience point expenditure (see [2.5]), since entire fields of knowledge are considerably more complex than individual situations or items.

If a character wishes to use a field he/she has not finished studying (but has at least half completed), or has previously failed, the GM should assign a modifier equal to the field's NFM - (half the number of years required to learn the skill); Do not adjust this number for memory, etc..

Time spent in boring circumstances, ego on spacecraft, should count double towards time spent studying fields, while time spent adventuring, and so forth, should not count at all. A character who takes time off to study should be able to learn one field twice as quickly (perhaps only if he/she makes a WP roll) or two fields concurrently.

[2.7] Psionics Psionics in FS are presented for those who wish to use them, not as an integral part of the implied Game-World, but as a module which can be added to it painlessly. H you want to make psionics integral to the Game-World, either give them to everybody in some degree (eg. make them available to everybody, exactly like normal skills), or tie them to communications, faster than light travel, or some other all-pervasive aspect of the environment.

Normally, a player character has a psionic skill if and only if it is so indicated under abilities and limitations. The GM may discuss with the player which ones he/she can have, or send word down from the mountain. The Exp value of skills reflects the cost to improve them, not to acquire them. In some places psionic characters are persecuted by society (especially if it can detect them), so players may eliminate them by paying the cost for removing any other ability or limitation.

A psionic task succeeds or fails normally, but using a psionic skill always costs the- psionic character fatigue equal to the OR of the attempt, and if a OR 10 was achieved, increases his/her wound level by one. H this cost exceeds the character's remaining fatigue, then the character automatically fails, and has his/her fatigue reduced to zero, or may take the excess fatigue loss as an increase in wound status, and die after the task is completed; a character can (deliberately) die this way.

Usually, the results of psionic tasks have a base duration, which determines how long the effects of the skill's application last. This duration may be doubled by expending additional fatigue equal to that lost in the attempt, tripled by spending twice that much, and so forth; but may not increase the duration to more than thirty times normal (this is a limitation on characters killing themselves, really).

Where applicable, tasks with targets more than 10km away suffer a -1 penalty, more than 100km away a further -1, etc., so that a task which affects a target 15000km away, suffers a net -4 penalty.

If this wasn't already obvious. the best way to generate an NPC is decide what attributes. skills. etc. such a character would possess. and give them to him/her. Finished. Don't bother about experience points· for NPCs either. If they survive, and the PCs bump into them again, maybe they've gotten better at sometl:ling, maybe they've been too busy.

<div style="text-align:right;font-size:.78em;opacity:.5;margin:.4em 0"><a href="/foresight-1986.pdf#page=27">ForeSight 1986 · p.27 ↗</a></div>

[ii] Wing it. If the situation calls for a plumber, then he/she's probably pretty reasonable at plumbing (Diagnose PCS-15; Repair PCS-20; knows Construction). If he/she has to do anything else, then he/she's probably poor to fair at it (no skill, to PCS.10, say). Just remember the following: a PCS of 10 is not particularly good; PCS-15 is okay; PCS-20 is pretty good; and PCS-25 or better represents expertise. Most NPCs will not engage in physical labour (a lot of plumbing will be done by robots, I expect, these are covered in ForeScene), and will have low Pain Resistance and Fatigue (say 10), similarly they will avoid combat (again say 10), while they will have average capacities for other common skills (Empathy, Search, Scan, and perhaps some vehicle at 15). If an NPC does something for a living, give him/her 20, or perhaps 25, in it. Simple. Do not
use these guidelines for all NPCs, just the vast
majority.

Also: the stats of most NPCs, especially minor ones, are generally irrelevant, and generic values can be assumed. A number of generic NPCs are supplied for your convenience, each simply requires appropriate fields of knowledge.

[iii] NPCs are not as good as PCs, at least in general. Certainly powerful villains whom the PCs have sought long and hard, should be more than a match for a solitary PC, or at least possessed of wicked henchpersons, but by and large, NPCs, having less initiative, and drive, than PCs, will possess lesser capabilities. Bear this in mind.

If an NPC is to be generated, the way a PC is, then give himlher base values of 3 in all attributes except AP (which remains 5). Otherwise generate the character as per normal (roll 06+2 rather than 06+4 if the semi-random method is desired). This system may be used by players who wish to generate their characters' spouses, siblings, etc. (subject to GM intervention: "Why does your husband have such a high Handguns skill, while you claim he works as a pharmacist?").

Apart from that, less space should be required to list an NPC's capabilities than a PC's, so a (more compact) NPC record is provided.

[2.91 Merits, Demerits, and
Notoriety (Optional)
If a character works for somebody, or is involved in some form of pecking order, then it is a good idea for the GM to keep some numerical record of his/her situation. In ForeSight a character's status in a hierarchy can be kept track of via merit points (MPs, if you like abbreviations). When a character is seen to perform some (contextually) meritorious act, he/she will gain merit points, and if he/she is seen to perform some unmeritorious act, he or she will lose merit points (or gain demerits). Note that inaction may, in some cases, constitute either a meritorious or (more probably) an unmeritorious one.

Example: a character who is a law enforcement officer might gain MPs for preventing an armed hold-up, successfully resolving a hostage situation, or rescuing a drowning person from a river; he/she might lose MPs for not attempting to do any of the above in appropriate circumstances, or displaying incom-petefICS or poor judgement in an attempt to do one of the above, or using unconventional and unsanc-tioned methods to do any of the above (especially if he/she fails).

Merit points are in general non-transferable: one's MPs gained as a policeman count for nothing if one becomes a spaceship pilot, but some proportion of one's merit points might be convertable to a related field: a paramedic might retain half of his/her merit points if he/she became a fire-fighter (and if he/she returned to being a paramedic, he/she could have the higher of his/her previous merit as a paramedic, and half his/her merit as a fire-fighter).

Merit points may accord a character benefits in terms of position and promotion, salary, choice of assignment, privileges, respect, medals, and/or trust (placed in himlher by his/her employer, and/or colleagues).

Guidelines for Merit Point Awards A character should be accorded merit points only for successful and constructive performance. An act within the line of duty, but beyond normal expectations, should be accorded 1 to 5 merit points. Eg. a policeman is expected to deal with a small scale hostage situation, but to resolve it without casualties or major mobilisation would be worth merit points.

An act within the line of duty, but beyond any expectation, should be accorded 6 to 20 merit points. Situations of this sort would occur most often when unforeseen events cause a character's superiors to knowingly allocate insufficient resources to a task, and the task is successfully performed anyway; and when there is more to an assignement than first appeared. Eg. a squad is assigned to seize an undefended" village which turns out to be defended by a platoon of veteran troops, and nevertheless prevails; or a mathematics student sits a first year exam in which his/her lecturers have placed a "Great Unsolved Problem· as a joke, and he/she solves it without realising the joke.

Notoriety Notoriety is a special case of MPs. A character gains notoriety for being believed (by law-enforcement agencies, the mass media, and/or the public) to have performed criminal acts. A character loses notoriety points for serving the prison sentences, or whatever, accorded to people who commit the crimes the character is seen to have perpitrated. Each point of notoriety is roughly equal to a year's imprisonment in today's terms, although how this works out will vary from place to place (eg. some places today put people in prison longer for harmless sexual eccentricity than for violent rape).

A given crime will accord notoriety points (NPs if you prefer) to the character suspected of having performed it roughly equal in number to the number of years of imprisonment a person today would expect to serve if tried for a similar offence in some contemporary western nation. Eg. 20 NPs for second degree murder, 5-10 NPs for armed robbery, 0.1 NPs for parkingltraffic violations. A crime whose NP total is less than one twentieth of a character's existing notoriety will not contribute to his/her notoriety (eg. a character with notoriety ~2 no longer acquires notoriety for parking violations).

A character's notoriety rank (see above) determines who is after himlher, and how badly they want himlher, and also, perhaps, the size of the reward for information leading to his/her arrest. Role-playing a character in a prison is likely to be pretty dull (unless, for some reason, the authorities allow him/her to roam free on some "prison planet").

Rank 0: A law abiding citizen I

Rank 1: Wanted in one place (ie. by one law enforcement body).

Rank 2: Wanted by one planetary law-enforcement body. Reward -1000 SVU.

Rank 3: Wanted in one stellar system, and pursued by one government while within that system. Reward - 20 OOOSVU.

Rank 4: Wanted by the Federation, pursued by agents of one system seeking extradition. Reward - 50 000 SVU.

Rank 5: Wanted and pursued by Federation (within the Federation). Reward - 100 000 SVU.

Rank 6: Wanted and pursued by Federation (within and without the Federation). Reward - 250 000 SVU.

Rank 7: IPAC (Interplanet Police Assistance Company) has been contracted to terminate the character. Reward - 500 000 SVU.

Rank 8: The Department of Justice Executive Agency (Covert, Wet Operations) has assigned a top operative to the character's case. Reward - 1000 000 SVU.

Rank 9: Mass media coverage of the character's exploits have made his/her face widely recognised. Maniac amateur bounty hunters dream of taking the character out. Reward - 2000 000 SVU.

Rank 10: A legend in his/her own slime, the character is hunted under his/her aliases as well (who are individually accorded the status of badly disliked skeet), and will probably continue to be after his/her own (actual) death.

The above are examples, of course, and may not apply in specific instances, but I expect you get the idea.
