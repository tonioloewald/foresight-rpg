# Armor
<!--{ "parent": "world", "order": 4 }-->

*One-pager. Armor is an [object](/equipment/) that changes how [Damage](/damage/) lands on you. It doesn't make you harder to hit, and at the top end it doesn't make the hits smaller either — it makes most of them **not have happened**.*

**Protection** is listed by damage type: `M3 I4` turns melee attacks as if the DC were 3 lower and bullets as if it were 4 lower — and a type with **no number is no protection**, which is why a gambeson is no help at all against a rifle. **Absorb (A)** is a different thing and a much stronger one: it comes off the **damage**, after the roll, stepping the result down **A** notches — two wound levels to one, one level to a bare stun check, a stun check to nothing. A good hit dilutes Protection and **cannot touch Absorb**, and `nP` doesn't touch it either; the only answer to Absorb is a bigger blow. So it is the mark of a **rigid** shell — the soft armors have none, and against anything with an `A` at all, *half the hits that land stop counting* (see [Damage](/damage/)).

**Penalty** is what wearing it costs (an ease-factor hit to Coordination in a heavy suit, to Perception under a full helm). Heavier isn't strictly better: it's a trade of protection against penalty, weight, and price — and against the [Technology Level](/technology-levels/) that can make or repair it.

## How you beat a man in armor

Good armor doesn't take the edge off a blow, it takes the blow out of the fight — a swarm of attackers who cannot get past `A` are not slowly wearing him down, they are doing **nothing at all**, however many of them connect. So the way to beat him is not to swing more; it is to **change the question**:

- **Wear him out.** He is carrying twenty kilos of steel and swinging a weapon in it. A long mêlée is an exhaustion check with the magnitude of the effort as its modifier ([Exhaustion](/exhaustion/)), and the suit is part of that magnitude. He isn't hurt — he's **spent**, and a Spent (3) opponent is at −3 on everything, which is how you finally land something.
- **Go for the gaps.** Visor, armpit, groin, the back of the knee. **Call the shot** for an up-front ease-factor penalty and the success *is* the hit on the gap — no armor rating applies to a place the armor isn't. Every joint is an adjective, and a dagger through the visor has killed better men than you.
- **Take him off his feet.** Grapple, trip, drag him down, pin him ([Melee Combat](/melee-combat/)). Damage never enters it, and in armor he gets up slowly.
- **Bring the right tool.** A crossbow, a war pick, a lance. Penetration beats Protection; a big DC beats Absorb.

*Designer's Note — why the D&D answer is wrong, and we can name the evidence. In the AC model a swing is full damage or nothing, so a man in plate facing a crowd dies by attrition: roll enough times and the small chance gets through, at full value, over and over. History says the opposite. Spanish companies in plate and mail fought enormous forces armed with stone and obsidian, killed on the scale of hundreds, and took **negligible** casualties — not because they dodged, but because steel was not penetrable by what was being swung at it. That is the shape ForeSight reproduces: the swarm's blows fall off the bottom of the ladder, and nothing happens. It is also, note, the same asymmetry the whole game is built to produce — one highly effective person against five ordinary ones — arriving here through equipment instead of Score. And it is not invulnerability: those companies **did** lose men, to exhaustion, to being unhorsed and cut off, to missiles finding gaps, to being dragged down and taken alive. Every one of those is a rule above. Armor's job is to remove damage as the axis of the fight; the fight then has to be won on the other axes, which is exactly where we want the friction to be.*
